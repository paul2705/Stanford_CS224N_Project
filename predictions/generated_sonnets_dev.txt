Thine eyes I love, and they, as pitying me,
Knowing thy heart torment me with disdain,
Have put on black, and loving mourners be,
To look upon thee in that black night.
How many a holy and obsequious tear
Hath dear religious love stol'n from mine eye,
As interest of the dead, which now appear
But things removed that hidden in thee lie.
Thou art the grave where buried love doth live,
Hung with the trophies of my lovers gone,
Who all their parts of me to thee did give;
That due of many now is thine alone.
  Their images I loved I view in thee,
  And thou, all they, hast all the all of me.

Beshrew that heart that makes my heart to groan
For that deep wound it gives my friend and me.
Is 't not enough to torture me alone,
When thou thyself dost torment me with thoughts of thee?
Ah, if I lose thee, my life hath but one end,
And that is this: to be your prey,
To be stol'n from yourself when you are dead.
O, what a torment wouldst thou prove
Were it not thy sour leisure gave sweet leave
To entertain the time with thoughts of love,
Which time and thoughts so sweetly doth deceive,
  And that thou teachest how to make one twain
  By praising him here who doth hence remain.
  O, praise him here whose body lies hid,



2
So, now I have confessed that he is thine
And I myself am mortgaged to thy will,
Myself I'll forfeit, so that other mine
May love and be beloved of thee.
For how can I live when thou art gone,
When thou art my all the world and mine alone?
O, therefore, love, be of good cheer;
I cannot forbear praising thee till I've seen
Thy face painted in living color on me.
Then may I dare to boast how I do love thee;
Even so, love, be of good cheer; I'll confess
That thou dost make my heart beat faster.
  Then may I dare to say that thou art true
  And that I love him more than myself.



3
Whoever hath her wish, thou hast thy will,
And will to boot, and will in overplus.
More than enough am I that vex thee still,
Since thou dost use me not for my sake,
But for thyself's sake deceivest all the world.
Thou art as tyrannous now as when first I saw thee,
Or as affable a jester now as then;
For well thou know'st to my dear doting heart
That love is more blessed than wealth or birth.
Yet be blamed if ever I see thee frown,
For 'twixt thy sweet self and mine there is no middle place,
Nor canst thou make me happier than thou art.
  But do thy worst, my love; I cannot help it.
  Thou gav'st me thine not to give back again.



4
If thy soul check thee that I come so near,
Swear to thy blind soul that I was thy will,
And will, thy soul knows, is admitted there.
Then need I not fear the worst of wrongs
When in the least of them my life hath end;
I see a better state to me belongs
Than that which on thy humor doth depend.
Thou canst not vex me with inconstant mind,
Since that my life on thy revolt doth lie.
O, what a happy title do I find,
Happy to have thy love, happy to die!
  But what's so blessed-fair that fears no blot?
  Thou mayst be false, and yet I know it not.
  Thou canst not vex me with inconstant mind,



5
Thou blind fool, Love, what dost thou to mine eyes
That they behold and see not what they see?
They know what beauty is, see where it lies,
And that it lives in you, within your cheek.
Yet do thy worst, love; I warrant thee
Thyself art as black as night, or rather blacker,
For eye and heart are of the same hue,
Or at least so I think; but upon this score
I am false, for my judgment knows no color.
  For eyes and hearts are of two hues:
  The one red, the other white.
  And yet on this score I am false,
  Because I have looked into thine eyes, and there found
  No such thing as red nor any like thereof.



6
When my love swears that she is made of truth
I do believe her though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Made tongue-tied by time's tyranny,
Speaking in the accents of children.
But when she tells me that she loves me so
And hath not altered a word since we first met,
My fears are realized; for although she be true,
She knows it not for myself but for me,
Which makes our love stronger and more steadfast.
  Then, if she be false, well assured I am
  That she hears not my prayers nor mine own heart.
  But if she be true, well assured I am
  That she hears my every groan and sigh.

8
Be wise as thou art cruel; do not press
My tongue-tied patience with too much disdain,
Lest sorrow lend me words, and words express
Thy true thoughts more truly than thy outward show.
For if the outward show be false,
The inward man in thee lies buried,
And all those beauties whereof thou dost make
Are but shadows of what they should be.
Were it not sinful on my part to frown,
To keep silence when others would speak?
Had I not sinned against myself thus,
By forbearing to speak in thy praise,
When others might wish to hear what I had to say?
  For to no other pass my verses tend



9
In faith, I do not love thee with mine eyes,
For they in thee a thousand errors note;
But 'tis my heart that loves what they despise,
And for this love there is no end.
In it's bosom all hearts are knit,
Of many and of few, rich and poor,
Happy or sad, strong or weak,
All-loving or all-neglecting;
And for this love there is no end.
  For as the waves make towards the pebbled shore,
  So do my thoughts, ever changing place,
  Towards thee and away from thee.
  Thus do I pine and surfeit day by day,
  Or gluttoning on all, or all away.



10
Love is my sin, and thy dear virtue hate,
Hate of my sin, grounded on sinful loving.
O, but with mine compare thou thine own state,
And in that bewailed guilt nothing at all;
Till whatsoever part of thee thou lov'st belongs
To that which thou distils as thy sweet self doth live,
Making no distinction between friend and foe.
Thou art the better part of me, myself hated,
Whilst I, whom thou deserv'st so graciously,
Beggared of mine own good will to thank thee.
  But do not so. I love thee in such sort
  As, thou being mine, mine is thy good report.
  Then do not so. I love thee in such sort
  As, thou being mine, mine is thy bad report.



11
Lo, as a careful huswife runs to catch
One of her feathered creatures broke away,
Sets down her babe, and makes all swift dispatch
Of that which she sought; so I, made lame by fortune's woe,
Take my leave of this world, and return home.
For where I abide, sickness doth almost kill me,
And life no longer keeps pace with decay;
Therefore, like a sad widow, I stay at home,
Looked on but as an after-dowry;
  And time, who gives the better part of me,
  Leads me still farther off from thee,
  Where thou art not, though yet I fear lest I die.
  Then should I beguile myself with thoughts of love,
  When heaven itself shall lead me away hence.
