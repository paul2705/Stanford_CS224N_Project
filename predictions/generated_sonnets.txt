To me that languished for her sake;

Poor soul, the center of my sinful earth,
Pressed with these rebel powers that thee array,
Why dost thou pine within and suffer dearth,
Wishing thine own sweet self to dwell in me?
Till I bring forth another thee from thy brain,
And she, too, like a mare in heat,
Shall neigh no hoof but hiss; then canst thou enjoy
Thyself as thy sweet self would be if thou were dead.
Look what an unthrift in the world doth spend
Shifts but his place, for still the world enjoys it;
But beauty's waste hath in the world an end,
And, kept unused, the user so destroys it.
  No love toward others in that bosom sits
  That on himself such murd'rous shame commits.



2
My love is as a fever, longing still
For that which longer nurseth the disease,
Feeding on that which doth preserve the ill,
Which, once gone, cannot be retrieved.
I may not evermore see thee,
Lest my very presence should make thee sick;
But thou gav'st me thine not to give back again,
Nor didst forsake me when I was least needy.
O, what a mansion have those vices got
That for their habitation chose out thee,
Where beauty's veil doth cover every blot,
And all things turns to fair that eyes can see!
  Take heed, dear heart, of this large privilege;
  The hardest knife ill used doth lose his edge.

O me, what eyes hath love put in my head,
Which have no correspondence with true sight!
Or if they have, where is my judgment fled,
That I may not praise that which is not seen?
Be thou the tenth muse, ten times more in worth
Than those old nine which rhymers invocate;
And he that calls on thee, let him bring forth
Eternal numbers to outlive long date.
I grant, sweet love, your argument holds good
Against mine own clear vision; but how do you prove
My love to be greater than yours, when I see
In others' eyes such a discrepancy?
  O, know, sweet love, I can show you no reason
  But that your love is greater than mine already.

Canst thou, O cruel, say I love thee not
When I against myself with thee partake?
Do I not think on thee when I forgot thee,

Happy to have found such a one as thou art?
No, let me be obsequious in thy sight,
And take thou my oblation, poor but free,
Which is not mixed with seconds, knows no art
But mutual render, only me for thee.
Thou gav'st me thine not to give back again;
Therefore, like a true lover, dost thou keep.
  If I lose thee, thou gav'st me nothing;
  If I win thee, thou gav'st me much.
  Then thank me not for that which I do receive,

O, from what power hast thou this powerful might
With insufficiency my heart to sway?
To make me give the lie to my true sight,
Or to persuade me that thou art greater
Than I can show by thy deeds to prove it.
Be where you list, my love, and mine eye shall see;
But do not so, for 't lies in me
To believe that thou dost more than thou show'st.
O absence, what a torment wouldst thou prove

To entertain the time with thoughts of love,
Which time and thoughts so sweetly doth deceive,
  And that thou teachest how to make one twain

8
Cupid laid by his brand and fell asleep.
A maid of Dian's this advantage found,
And his love-kindling fire did quickly steep
In sweet savour to her new acquaintance;
But when she saw the perfumed tincture
Of his breath on her hand, she fainted away,
Beggared of his aid, and left him bewailed.
The next day he called upon her again,
And said unto her, "Thou art as fair
As any mother's child, though not so bright
As those gold candles fixed in heaven."
Then said the maiden sadly to him,
"Since thou dost believe me thus ill,
My life hath but one purpose: to please thee."



9
The little love-god, lying once asleep,
Laid by his side his heart-inflaming brand,
Whilst many nymphs that vowed chaste life to keep
In secret communion with his sweet face
Mourned him as they would have died for him.
Then, if memory serves me rightly,
The goddesses of the dead laid hold on his hand
And invoked his aid in their wailing;
But, fearing the anger of heaven,
They fled from the sight of men's eyes,
Save one, whose beauty and virtue alone
Resembled his own sweet form in death's shade.
  Then said the goddesses: "This man was our friend;
  But since he is dead, we mourn for him no more."



10
When my love swears that she is made of truth,
I do believe her, though I know she lies,
That she might think me some untutored youth,
Made tongue-tied by time's tyranny,
Speaking for myself and for no one else.
But when she tells me that she loves me so
And hath in me all the fullness of hers,
My first thought is not 'Tis true,
But, 'tis said, 'tis better to be false
Than live in a world where truth cannot thrive.
  To be false well is worse than death,
  For who knows what hell may await us both?
  Thou gav'st me thine not to give back again.
  If thou wilt leave me, let it be now;



11
Two loves I have, of comfort and despair,
That like two spirits do suggest me still.
My better angel is a man right fair,
Whose worth's unknown to my love, though it seemeth to me so.
Another loves I have, which yet in me doth lie,
A crow that flies in heaven's sweetest air;
Yet as he sings praises of thee, so doth mine own voice say
To the very same thing: "Thy beauty delights in praise."
So then my better angel sings of thee,
And all the angels that sing with him are false.
  But thy beauty delights in mine, and mine in thee delights also.
  How many lambs might the stern wolf betray
  If like a lamb he could his looks translate.
  Thou gav'st me thine not to give back again.
